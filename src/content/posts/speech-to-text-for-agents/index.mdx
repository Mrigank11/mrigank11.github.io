These days I talk to my coding agents more than I type to them. A good prompt has the goal, the constraints, what I already tried and the error I'm staring at. That's a paragraph, and typing it is slow, so I used to write short prompts and let the agent guess the rest.

Speaking fixes that. I talk a lot faster than I type, rambling is fine (models handle messy input well), and my eyes can stay on the diff while I talk.

I do this dozens of times a day, so it has to be instant and cheap. And these prompts are full of code, file paths and internal stuff I'd rather not send to someone else's server. So I wanted it local.

In this post, I compare two free local apps, **Handy** and **FluidVoice**. One result surprised me.

## Cloud solutions

The obvious option is a cloud app. You hold a key, speak, and clean text shows up at your cursor. Wispr Flow is the best-known one, and its UX set the bar for everything after it.

But:

- it's a monthly fee
- it stops working when your internet does
- your audio goes to someone else's servers

That last one matters more for agents than for normal dictation, because of what you end up saying out loud: file names, code, internal details.

## Local apps

Whisper and Parakeet are now small and fast enough to run on a laptop. No monthly fee, and it works offline.

What I wanted from one:

- a global hotkey, push-to-talk, and text pasted at the cursor
- light, since it sits in the background all day
- quick, since even a small delay breaks the flow of talking

## The field

There are a lot of free apps now. I looked at five:

<FieldGrid />

The three I set aside:

- **OpenWhispr** does a lot: dictation, meeting notes, even an agent you can talk to. Free, offline, on Windows, Mac and Linux. But it's Electron. Shipping a whole browser just to wait for a keypress felt like too much, and it felt slow too. I like my dictation apps a little rusty.
- **OpenSuperWhisper** is decent. Free, Mac only, and you can pick from several on-device models. It's fairly new though, and the others offered more.
- **VoiceInk** isn't free. You pay once, $25 to $49 depending on how many Macs, with a free trial and the source public. It runs locally, Apple Silicon only (there's an iOS app too). I passed because it's paid.

<FieldMatrix />

## Handy

Handy is free and open source, and works offline on Windows, Mac and Linux. **If you're not on a Mac, try this one.**

It's simple: mic, Silero VAD to drop the silence, Whisper or Parakeet, paste at the cursor. Whisper uses the GPU if there is one, Parakeet V3 is optimized for the CPU.

<LocalPipeline />

The backend (audio, VAD, inference) is Rust and the UI is React on Tauri, so no bundled Chromium. That's mainly why it made my list.

> Handy [isn't trying to be the fanciest app](https://github.com/cjpais/Handy). They want a simple codebase that's easy to fork and build on.

## FluidVoice

FluidVoice is also free and open source, but Mac only. It's a Swift app built on FluidAudio, an SDK that runs the models on the Apple Neural Engine, so the CPU and GPU stay free for everything else.

[FluidAudio's own numbers](https://github.com/FluidInference/FluidAudio/blob/a8f1482357748ba5487d07a1d25025c22818b7dc/Documentation/Benchmarks.md) on an M4 Pro: Parakeet TDT 0.6B v3 runs at 207x real time (RTFx) on English, and gets 2.5% average word error rate on LibriSpeech test-clean (two separate benchmark runs, same chip).

**Note**: The app is open source, but the formatting model that cleans up your text is a private runtime. On Intel Macs, only the Whisper models work.

## GPU vs Apple Neural Engine

The real difference between the two apps is where the model runs.

The GPU works almost everywhere and runs most models as they are. But it's shared, so a local LLM or a big build will compete with your dictation.

The Neural Engine is dedicated silicon: low power, nothing to fight with. But it's Apple only, and models have to be converted to Core ML first. On paper it's the better fit for something that's always on, and that's what I expected going in.

<GpuVsAne />

| | GPU | Apple Neural Engine |
|---|---|---|
| Works on | Almost any machine | Apple Silicon only |
| Models | Almost any | Core ML conversions |
| Contention | Shared with everything else | None |
| Power (on paper) | Higher | Very low |
| Best for | Windows, Linux, mixed setups | Always-on dictation on a Mac |

> If you like Rust, there's a Rust binding for FluidAudio too (`fluidaudio-rs`).

## Handy vs FluidVoice

I ran both side by side, and FluidVoice used more battery than Handy. On the Neural Engine! The opposite of what I expected.

Energy per minute of speech:

<EnergyPerMinute />

That's about 3.4x. I'm going per minute because the runs weren't the same length (79.1 s of speech for Handy, about 84 s for FluidVoice), and it's the most conservative way to put it.

Where the power goes:

<PowerBreakdown />

FluidVoice's CPU averaged 1.27 W, against 0.29 W for Handy. Moving the model to the Neural Engine didn't take the CPU out of the picture. And the Neural Engine alone drew 0.65 W, more than Handy's CPU, GPU and Neural Engine combined (0.42 W). FluidVoice did use less GPU (0.03 W vs 0.13 W), but that's small next to the rest.

**Note**: This is one run per app, not a benchmark, and it compares whole apps (app, model and chip together). I didn't log which model each one ran that day, so I can't say how much of the gap is silicon and how much is the model.

<Collapsible kicker="Measurement notes" title="How I measured this, and the raw numbers">

| Metric | Handy | FluidVoice |
|---|---|---|
| Spoken audio | 79.1 s | ~84 s |
| Avg CPU power | 0.29 W | 1.27 W |
| Avg GPU power | 0.13 W | 0.03 W |
| Avg Neural Engine power | 0 W | 0.65 W |
| Combined CPU/GPU/ANE | 0.42 W | 1.94 W |
| Measured energy | 0.0127 Wh | 0.0459 Wh |
| Energy per minute spoken | ~0.0096 Wh/min | ~0.0328 Wh/min |

Energy per minute is the measured energy divided by minutes of speech. Combined is CPU, GPU and Neural Engine power added up. One run each, and I didn't write down the exact tool or capture window, so treat these as directional.

Per minute gives 3.4x, raw measured energy gives 3.6x, and combined power gives 4.6x. I went with per minute since it accounts for the different run lengths.

One thing doesn't add up. Handy's 0.42 W over 79.1 s is about 0.0092 Wh, not the 0.0127 Wh in the table. FluidVoice's does (1.94 W x 84 s is about 0.045 Wh, close to 0.0459 Wh). I don't know why. Maybe the capture window ran past the speech, or something outside those three rails got counted. I've left the numbers as I recorded them.

</Collapsible>

So the Apple Neural Engine doesn't guarantee lower battery use, at least in my testing. I also really liked Handy's UX. It's very easy to use and fast, so I'm using it to draft this blog post as well.
